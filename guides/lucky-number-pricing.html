The last digits of a price are not neutral. Shoppers read them — consciously or not — as signals of value, quality and even luck. Here is how to use that, ethically and profitably.

1. Charm pricing (.99 and .95)

Dropping a price from 20.00 to 19.99 exploits the left-digit effect: we anchor on the first digit we read. Major e-commerce education resources describe charm pricing as the most widely taught psychological pricing tactic, and it remains standard in Western retail. It works best for value-positioned products and impulse purchases.

2. The lucky 8 ending

In Chinese, 8 (bā) sounds like 发 (fā), to prosper. Research summarised in Psychology Today found 8-endings over-represented in prices across China, Hong Kong, Malaysia and Japan, and a Macau hospitality study found 8-ending prices widely used by integrated resorts — notably on regular, not just discounted, prices. In these markets ¥19.8, ¥36.8, 168 or 888 can feel both attractive and auspicious.

3. Prices to avoid

  • Anything containing 4 when selling to Chinese-speaking buyers.
  • 250 (an insult) and 748 (rude).
  • Odd funeral-associated amounts for celebratory products.

4. Prestige (round) pricing

Luxury and premium brands often prefer round numbers — 200, 1,000 — which signal confidence and quality rather than bargain. Use round prices when you want buyers to think “premium,” not “deal.”

5. Seasonal love-code pricing

Around May 20, Valentine’s Day and Qixi, prices such as 5.20, 13.14, 52.0, 131.4, 520 or 1314 carry a built-in message. Brands use them for limited bundles, gift cards and couples’ packages. They are memorable, shareable and naturally fit social posts.

6. A simple testing plan

  1. Pick one high-traffic product.
  2. Run the current price against one alternative ending (e.g. 19.99 vs 19.88) for two to four weeks.
  3. Measure conversion rate and gross margin, not revenue alone.
  4. Roll out the winner, then test the next ending.
